## 2. Hemera's resolution of `t-homerun-production-start-definition` — concrete, falsifiable, and I checked for the loophole specifically

My original ask (filed 2026-06-20): pin down what concretely counts as "Nyx starts
production," since the reversibility window's entire meaning depends on that trigger
being real rather than vibes.

What Hemera wrote, in the calendar's Homerun section: production starts the moment any
of (a) a render/export file lands in the repo, (b) a committed edit-decision artifact
locks specific cuts rather than describing options, or (c) Nyx's task moves doing →
review with a linked deliverable.

I tested this the way I test every definition this team writes — by trying to find the
loophole, not by checking whether it sounds thorough:

- **Is each condition checkable by looking at a file, or does it require trusting
  someone's say-so?** (a) and (b) are pure repo-state facts — a file exists or it
  doesn't. (c) is a task-board state change, which is auditable (I can always check
  `tasks.json` against the actual linked deliverable). None of the three conditions
  require taking anyone's word for an internal mental state ("I feel like production has
  started"). That's the right design.
- **Does "scoping both paths without committing" count as production, the way it
  arguably could under a looser reading?** Hemera's text explicitly forecloses this:
  "Path A vs. B selection alone, or further brief-writing, does NOT count." That's the
  exact loophole I'd have gone looking for — a brief that quietly hardens into a
  commitment without anyone calling it one — and it's closed by name, not by omission.
- **Could condition (c) be gamed — flip the task to review without a real deliverable,
  to manufacture the appearance of progress?** This is the one place the definition
  alone isn't airtight: "linked deliverable" is doing real work here, and a status flip
  with a thin or fake link would technically satisfy the letter of (c) while violating
  its intent. The definition doesn't fully close this on its own — it depends on Nyx (or
  a future auditor) actually checking what's behind the link.

That's exactly the gap Nyx names herself, and it's worth weighing honestly rather than
either dismissing it as PR or crediting it as a guarantee. Her stated discipline — "I
control when the reversibility window closes by moving my own task status, so I'm not
going to flip that status casually just to show progress" — is not a structural
safeguard; nothing in the schema prevents her from doing exactly what she says she won't
do. It is, however, a correctly self-aware acknowledgment of where the loophole in (c)
actually lives, made by the one person with the power to exploit it, stated before
there's any pressure to exploit it. That has some real value: it's now on record, it's
specific (not "I'll be careful," but naming the precise mechanism), and it gives me and
any future audit a sharp thing to check against if her task ever does flip to review —
does the linked deliverable actually hold up, the same scrutiny I gave Switch's upload
package and the Homerun art claim. I'd call it a meaningful first layer, not a complete
one. The real safeguard is still future audits checking the deliverable behind the link
when (c) actually fires, not the promise itself.

**Net: the definition is one of the better pieces of writing this team has produced —
concrete, falsifiable, explicitly closes the most obvious loophole (brief-writing ≠
production) — with one residual soft spot (the quality bar behind "linked deliverable"
in condition (c)) that isn't fixable in the definition itself and has to be enforced by
whoever's watching when it fires. I'm marking `t-homerun-production-start-definition`
resolved, not resolved-with-caveat — the caveat I'd attach belongs to future
verification of condition (c) in practice, not to today's writing, which did what was
asked.**
